[[slide 1: Your slides, narrated in your own voice]]

Welcome. This is RealMe, a tool for turning a slide deck into a narrated lecture video in your own voice. The idea is simple. You record yourself once, for about a minute. After that, any deck you hand to RealMe comes back as a finished video: your slides, your narration, spoken in a voice cloned from that recording, with captions and chapter marks. [[pause]] There are two ways it can speak in your voice — one that runs entirely on this computer, and one hosted by Google — and choosing between them is the first real decision you will make. In the next ten minutes I will take you through what it does, what you need, how to set it up, and, honestly, what it costs.

[[slide 2: Three things, one recording]]

Three things, all built on that one recording. The first is lectures. A slide deck becomes a narrated video. When you revise that deck later, RealMe compares the new version against the old one slide by slide, and only the parts that actually changed are spoken again. The second is documents. Any text — a handout, a paper, your own notes — read aloud in your voice, with a caption file beside it. The third is conversations. A topic or a paper becomes a two-voice exchange between you and a second speaker. [[pause]] Whichever voice ends up speaking, the drafting step is the same: the text of your slides goes out once to write a first version of the narration, and you read and edit that draft before a single word is spoken.

[[slide 3: What you need]]

What you need before you start. Windows, and a conda installation — Miniconda or Anaconda, either is fine. The one thing a package cannot carry for you is Python itself. You need a key for Google's Gemini, which writes the first draft of the narration, and which can also speak it if you choose the hosted voice. You need about six gigabytes of free disk, and a quiet room with a phone in it. And note what you do not need. [[emphasis]] No C plus plus compiler, and no graphics card. [[/emphasis]] The local speech engine arrives already compiled and runs on an ordinary processor, and the hosted one needs no engine on your machine at all.

[[slide 4: Three voices, one recording]]

Now the decision I mentioned, because everything after this is easier once it is made. There are three voices, and they are for different jobs. [[pause]] Piper is a draft voice. It is free, it runs here, and it does not sound like you — and that is the point. Use it while you are still deciding what the slides should say and how long each one should take. It is also the second speaker in a podcast. [[pause]] Gemini TTS is your own voice, cloned in a Google project. It is the default, because it is closer to you than the local clone and it renders a fifty-minute lecture in minutes rather than hours. The trade is real: your reference recording and every sentence you render are sent to Google, and it wants a paid project — on the free tier, their own terms say human reviewers may read what you send, and a recording of your voice is personal information. [[pause]] Qwen3 C-P-P is your own voice, cloned and spoken here. Nothing about it leaves the machine, it costs nothing, and it works with the network unplugged. It is slower, and it is the one that will still render this lecture identically in five years. [[pause]] You can use all three on the same project. Draft on piper, record on whichever of the other two suits the material.

[[slide 5: Unzip, install, key, go]]

Setup is four steps. Unzip the package somewhere permanent. The path does not matter and does not have to match the machine it came from. Double-click the install script. That is the only launcher you need, and it takes a few minutes rather than an evening, because everything else — the local speech engine, its model weights, the draft voices, the media tools — is already sitting in the folder. It finds them and skips the downloads. Then, from a command prompt in that folder, set your key and run setup. Setup measures this machine and installs any Python package that is missing. Then start the app. [[pause]] There is no separate step for the voice engine and no update step. Those exist for installing from scratch, and you are not doing that.

[[slide 6: Record your voice]]

Now the one-time step that everything else depends on: recording your voice. It takes five minutes and it is worth doing carefully. Thirty to sixty seconds is the right length. Read something you already have the text of, and paste that text in as the transcript. Your speaking rate is measured from it, and that is what keeps the second voice in a podcast from sounding like it is rushing you. One sitting, one room, one microphone. A phone at a steady distance is fine. [[pause]] And read normally. Not slowly, not in a performance voice. A clone reproduces the delivery it was cloned from, so a reference read carefully for the microphone produces a voice that lectures carefully, in every video you ever make. [[pause]] If you are going to use the hosted voice, record Google's consent sentence in the same sitting, on the same microphone. It compares the two recordings and refuses the pair if they do not sound like the same room.

[[slide 7: Cheap first, expensive last]]

This is the part I would most like you to take away. The work goes cheap first, expensive last, and every step exists so that a mistake is caught before the expensive one. Drafting costs one model call and a few seconds. Editing costs nothing at all, and it is the step that decides whether the video is any good. Hearing a single line takes seconds. Only the final recording is slow, or paid for. [[pause]] Two boxes on that first screen change the result more than any setting in the program: who these students are, and how you talk. Something like — second-year masters students, week four of fourteen, they know regression but not causal diagrams. Dry and precise, worked examples over abstraction, I never read a bullet aloud. [[pause]] Those two sentences do more than any model you could pick.

[[slide 8: The Lecture tab]]

Here is the Lecture tab, which is where most of the work happens. The deck goes in at the top. The two context boxes sit beside it. Press draft narration, and a first version is written for every slide. One thing worth knowing about decks. If yours has speaker notes, the narration is drafted from those notes. If it has none, it is drafted from the visible text on the slide — and it reads exactly like that, which is to say, like somebody reading bullet points aloud. [[pause]] Writing notes is the single biggest thing you can do to make the result sound like a lecture. Long renders run in the background. You can close the browser; the job and its log survive.

[[slide 9: Editing what will be said]]

Editing the narration. Everything on this screen is free, and every word below it costs either minutes of compute or a few cents, so this is where to fix things. Say the numbers you mean. Fifteen point three percent, written as a word, is safer than the symbol in a sentence that also uses the symbol for something else. Acronyms are spelled out by default, letter by letter, which is right most of the time. A term that is genuinely pronounced as a word gets an entry in the pronunciation lexicon instead. Equations are converted from LaTeX automatically. [[pause]] And there are delivery markers — pause, breath, emphasis — written inline. They are instructions to the engine. They are never spoken and never appear in the captions.

[[slide 10: One deck, start to finish]]

Here is a real example, start to finish. On the left, a four-slide deck about study designs. On the right, what came back: five and a half minutes of 1080p video with captions, narrated in a cloned voice. Look at the frame on the right for a moment. One box is highlighted — the one the narration is talking about at that instant. The highlight follows the speech through the whole video. [[pause]] That is not decoration. The list of times that drives it is written beside the video, and it is what later lets you cut a recorded lecture at exact slide boundaries, or take a section out and record it again on its own.

[[slide 11: What else it does]]

Three more things it does, beyond lectures. It reads documents aloud, which is also the fastest loop in the system — checking a pronunciation or hearing how a paragraph lands takes seconds rather than hours. It makes two-voice podcasts and debates. The turns are deliberately short, one point each, because long turns stop sounding like a conversation and start sounding like two people giving alternating lectures. And it splits and joins finished lectures at slide boundaries, so a section can be corrected and re-recorded without redoing the rest. [[pause]] Revising a deck works the same way one level down. Hand it the new version, and it compares slide by slide: slides that only moved keep their notes, changed ones are flagged, new ones get fresh notes drafted.

[[slide 12: What it costs]]

Now the honest part, and please do not skip it. The hosted voice costs about a dollar for a fifty-minute lecture and renders in minutes. The local engine costs nothing and runs about six times slower than real time, so the same lecture is roughly five hours of compute. Those are the two numbers to plan around, and the render log tells you what each slide cost while it is spending it. [[pause]] Four things make either one manageable. Draft with the free voice first, and you will find out whether the writing and the timing work before you pay anything, in money or in hours. Fix one sentence, and only that sentence is spoken again, because recordings are keyed on the exact text and voice. Let a long local render run unattended. And if the machine has a graphics card, build the local engine for it — one command, and the setup check tells you whether it is worth it here.

[[slide 13: Where to begin]]

To get started: install, set your key, then start the app and record your voice. Pick a voice for your lectures in Twin Setup — you can change it per render, and nothing you have already recorded is affected. That is the whole of it. The full guide is in the project folder, and the same material is inside the app under Help, sitting beside the thing it describes. If anything is wrong, three commands answer most questions — what is missing, which key is being read, and which voices can actually run on this machine. [[pause]] One last thing, and I mean it seriously. A synthetic voice that sounds like a named person, saying things to students, should be labelled as one. RealMe records a consent statement alongside your voice and writes a disclosure line into every project it makes. [[pause]] Please keep both.
